# The Picard lab's other ageing papers, read (2026-11-14)

Written 2026-11-14 by a working session that John's planning session started, for the roadmap step
"read the Picard group's other ageing papers" (task `5058855c`). The reading of 2026-10-18
(`docs/readings/picard-lab-2026-10-18.md`) listed four papers from Martin Picard's group
(Mitochondrial Psychobiology Group, Columbia University) as seen by title only. This note reads each
and judges it by the same rule. **It proposes no change to any record, stage or estimate.**

## The rule applied

The same as on 2026-10-18 (`docs/record-schema.md`, the publications log): a paper is logged when it
bears on a treatment's test or on the headline question, which in practice means it reports a person
given a treatment and a measured result, it validates a tool that would say whether a repair worked,
or it changes what an entry's test or estimate rests on. A paper about how biology works, however
good, does not meet that, because nothing on the tree can move on it. The lowest rung of the
ladder above "idea only" is "shown in animals", so a result in cells grown in a dish cannot settle a
stage either.

## How each was read

Each paper was found in Europe PMC's index by its authors and journal, and its date, volume and pages
were taken from there. Three were read in their open copies at PubMed Central: the results, the
discussion and the authors' own limits, and the methods where a number needed them. The fourth is
behind a subscription at the publisher and has no open copy, so only its abstract was read; that is
enough to place a paper that, by its own abstract, proposes a model and reports no new measurement.

## What each paper found, and why it is not logged

**1. Sturm and others, "OxPhos defects cause hypermetabolism and reduce lifespan in cells and in
patients with mitochondrial diseases", Communications Biology 6: 22, 2023-01-12**
(https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-022-04303-x; open copy PMC9837150, read in full).
Two parts. First, the group pooled data from 17 published groups of patients with inherited
mitochondrial diseases (690 patients, 225 healthy controls). Patients used more energy at rest: their
oxygen use at rest, measured in six of the groups, was on average 30 per cent higher than controls',
their resting heart rate about 11 per cent higher, and their body mass index about 10 per cent lower.
The paper sets this beside reports that adults with these diseases die three to four decades early.
Second, skin cells from three patients with a fault in one energy-making gene (SURF1), and cells from
three healthy donors given a low dose of a drug that blocks the same machinery (oligomycin), were grown
for about 150 days and measured throughout. Both used roughly twice the energy of healthy cells;
they stopped dividing after 40 to 53 per cent fewer divisions; they lost the protective caps on their
chromosomes (telomeres) 1.6 to 7.7 times faster per division; and DNA-mark "clocks" ran 54 to 131 per
cent faster per division. The authors name the small number of donors and the dish as the limits, and
say the energy figures in cells are estimated indirectly.
*Why not logged:* it tests no treatment and validates no tool. The patient half compares people with
a rare inherited disease to controls at one point in time; the cell half is below the ladder's first
rung. It is evidence for the group's argument that a cell under strain spends more energy and ages
faster, which bears on how the energy-and-fuel branch describes its subject (see the end of this
note), not on any entry's test.

**2. Bobba-Alves and others, "Cellular allostatic load is linked to increased energy expenditure
and accelerated biological aging", Psychoneuroendocrinology 155: 106322, 2023-06-14**
(https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psyneuen.2023.106322; open copy PMC10528419, the authors' manuscript,
read in full). Skin cells from three healthy donors were grown for their whole dividing life, some in
a stress hormone (dexamethasone, a drug that acts like cortisol) the whole time. The stressed cells
used about 62 per cent more energy, stopped dividing after about 20 per cent fewer divisions, lost
telomere length about 28 per cent faster per division (comparing each donor's
stressed and unstressed cells; about 9 per cent, and not clearly different, in the authors' main
model), and ran faster on two of five DNA-mark clocks
(about 33 and 36 per cent) with no clear change on the other three. Blocking the cells' fuel supply to
their power plants on top of the hormone made things worse, not better (a further 18.5 per cent fewer
divisions), which the authors read as total energy spent, not the power plants themselves, driving
the faster ageing. The authors say they cannot tell which way the link runs, that clocks are less
reliable in cells than in people, and that dividing skin cells may not behave like the non-dividing
cells of the brain; and they note that one of the three donors had died four days after birth of an
unknown cause, which may explain some of the spread between donors.
*Why not logged:* three donors' cells in a dish, and no treatment meant to slow ageing. It meets no
entry's test.

**3. Kurade and others, "Mitochondrial and psychosocial stress-related regulation of FGF21 in
humans", Nature Metabolism 7(11): 2212-2220, 2025-10-14**
(https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-025-01388-6; the authors' manuscript at PubMed Central, PMC13050275,
read in full). FGF21 is a hormone the body makes under fasting and metabolic strain, and it is
already used as a blood marker of inherited mitochondrial disease. In one study visit, 70 controls
and 40 patients with mitochondrial disease gave blood before and after a short public-speaking test
(the study is registered as NCT04831424). In controls the hormone fell by about 20 per cent within 20
minutes; in patients it rose by about 32 per cent. Fasting levels were nearly three times higher in
patients and rose with age in controls. The group then looked up the hormone in a large British health
study (UK Biobank, about 52,000 people), where higher levels went with divorce or separation, among
other things. The authors say the study was sized for its main questions, not for this hormone.
*Why not logged:* a stress-reaction study at one visit. It follows no one forward to death or
disease and tests no treatment, so the hormone is not, on this paper, a tool that could say whether a
repair worked. The 2026-10-18 reading left the group's blood measures (GDF15, cell-free mitochondrial
DNA) off the measuring branch for the same reason, and this paper does not change that.

**4. Shaulson, Cohen and Picard, "The brain-body energy conservation model of aging", Nature Aging
4: 1354-1371, 2024-10-08** (https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-024-00716-x; behind a subscription, no
open copy; abstract read in Europe PMC's index). A theory paper. It argues that as tissues collect
damage their cells spend more energy, the brain senses the extra demand through signals the cells
release, and it saves energy by turning down less urgent work, which the authors propose is what
shows up as the fatigue, inactivity, duller senses and hormone changes of old age.
*Why not logged:* a proposed model with no new measurement.

## What this means for the records

**No record changes.** None of the four is logged, no entry's stage or estimate moves, and no new
entry is proposed. Like the seven papers of 2026-10-18, they are careful work on how mitochondria and
the body's energy budget relate to ageing; none tests a treatment in an animal or a person, and none
follows people to an outcome.

**One thing for the question already waiting on John.** The roadmap holds a step, waiting since
2026-10-18, to ask Martin Picard to read the energy-and-fuel branch, starting with its sentence that
the cell's power plants "fail with age". Papers 1, 2 and 4 make the group's case plainer than the
2023 review did: in their account a strained cell's power plants do not simply fail, they work harder
and spend more, and the extra spending is what goes with faster ageing. That is a wording question
for John and Picard, and this note changes nothing on the branch.

**Newer papers seen, not read.** Europe PMC's index lists later papers from the group that were not
on the 2026-10-18 list and are not judged here: Shaulson and others, "Context-specific hypermetabolism
in humans with mitochondrial defects", Cell Press Blue, 2026-10-07; Liu and others, on immune-cell
mitochondria in mitochondrial disease, Neurology Genetics, 2026-01-02; Huang and others, on GDF15 in
saliva, Molecular Genetics and Metabolism, 2025-06-16; and Trumpff and others, on cell-free
mitochondrial DNA and acute stress, Psychoneuroendocrinology, 2025-06-06. From their titles all are
patient, cell or stress-reaction work of the same kind; a later reading can open them if John wants.
