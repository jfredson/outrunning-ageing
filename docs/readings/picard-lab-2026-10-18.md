# A reading of the Picard lab's papers on mitochondria and ageing (2026-10-18)

Written 2026-10-18 by a session that John's planning session started. John asked whether the work of Martin Picard's group (Mitochondrial Psychobiology Group, Columbia University) belongs in the project. This note answers it paper by paper. It changes no record: nothing under `data/` was touched, no publication is logged, no leaf is added. The one piece of new tree it offers, a proposed treatment-or-tool entry on the measuring branch, is written out below for John's ruling and is not in `data/`.

Every paper was opened at the publisher's page or its open copy at PubMed Central and read as text. What was read of each is said under it: the full text for most, the abstract and opening paragraphs for the Nature Metabolism review and the older-adults paper. The group's Substack posts were used only to find the papers. The 2025 preprint on "mitotypes" was not used for any fact. A second preprint on blood markers of mitochondrial health in ageing (bioRxiv, 2025-01-31) is still a preprint in Europe PMC's index and was not used either.

## The rule applied

The publications log takes a paper when it bears on a treatment's test or on the headline question, which in practice means one of three things: it reports a person given a treatment and a measured result, it validates a tool that would say whether a repair worked, or it changes what a leaf's test or estimate rests on. A paper that describes how biology works, however good, does not meet that, because nothing on the tree can move on it. None of the papers below met it, so none is logged. Each is listed with what it found, so the decision can be checked.

## What each paper found, and why it is not logged

**1. Zhou and others, PLOS Biology 22(8): e3002723, 2024-08-22** (https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002723). Pieces of mitochondrial DNA sometimes get copied into the cell's main DNA ("Numts"). The group counted new ones in 1,187 people's stored samples (798 brain, 389 blood) and found more in brain than blood, and about five times more in one brain region (the front of the cortex) than in the cerebellum. In people with no memory impairment, more insertions went with dying younger: about two more insertions per decade of life lost (r squared 0.094). The link was weaker in people with mild impairment and absent in people with Alzheimer's dementia. In cultured skin cells followed over time, one new insertion appeared about every 13 days, and cells from a patient with a mitochondrial gene fault gained them 4.7 times faster. The authors say themselves that the study is too small to map where in the genome the insertions land and that the rates they report may be a lower bound. (Read: abstract, the mortality results and the discussion.)
*Why not logged:* it is a correlation in stored post-mortem tissue and a cell-culture measurement. No treatment aims at these insertions and no tool built on them exists, so it meets no leaf's test and moves no estimate. The accompanying primer (Doss and Breuss, PLOS Biology 22(8): e3002756, 2024-08-23, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002756) says the practical importance of such small changes is still unclear, that the link with earlier death means either that the insertions harm or that they travel with other mitochondrial faults that do, and that whether they are a marker or a cause is for future studies to settle. The primer is commentary on the paper, not new evidence, and is not logged either.
*One thing worth a watch:* the damaged-DNA branch is "mutations that accumulate in a cell's DNA". If a later, larger or longitudinal study shows these insertions harm tissue (not only travel with earlier death), they would be a new kind of damage for that branch to name. Nothing today supports changing the branch.

**2. Sturm and others, Scientific Data 9: 751, 2022-12-03** (PubMed 36463290, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-022-01852-y; open copy PMC9719499). (Read: background, methods and the technical validation section.) A description of a public dataset: skin cells from six healthy donors and three people with a mitochondrial disease, grown until they stopped dividing, with many measurements taken along the way (DNA marks and "clocks", gene activity, mitochondrial DNA count, telomere length, whole genome), plus runs where the cells' energy supply was interfered with. The paper reports no finding about a treatment; its "technical validation" section only shows the measurements repeat.
*Why not logged:* a dataset in cultured cells. It tests no treatment and validates no tool for people.

**3. Trumpff and others, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 121(27): e2317673121, 2024-06-18** (open copy PMC11228499). In older adults who had been interviewed for years before death, higher well-being went with more of the cell's energy-making machinery (complex I) in a region of the brain, and more negative mood with less. The two kinds of experience together explained 18 to 25 per cent of the variation in that protein. Post-mortem tissue. (Read: abstract and introduction.)
*Why not logged:* a correlation between self-reported mood and brain protein after death. It tests no treatment and gives no tool a living person could use.

**4. Monzel, Enriquez and Picard, Nature Metabolism 5(4): 546-562, 2023-04-26** (https://doi.org/10.1038/s42255-023-00783-1; open copy PMC10427836). A proposal for naming the many things mitochondria do (properties of the cell, molecular features, activities, functions, behaviours), arguing that "mitochondrial function" and "mitochondrial dysfunction" are misleading because mitochondria are many-sided and change reversibly. Read: abstract and opening paragraphs.
*Why not logged:* a terminology proposal. It reports no measurement and no result.
*One thing for John to rule on (a wording question, not a fact):* the energy-and-fuel branch says the cell's power plants "fail with age", and the hallmark it is built on (López-Otín and others, 2023, already logged) is named "mitochondrial dysfunction". The review's argument is that "fail" is too simple a word. If John wants the branch's plain sentence softened (for example "change and wear with age"), that is a wording edit he can order; the schema keeps the hallmark's key as it is.

**5. Picard and others, Biological Psychiatry 84(1): 9-17, 2018** ("A mitochondrial health index sensitive to mood and caregiving stress", PMC6014908, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2018.01.012). (The lead said PNAS; it is Biological Psychiatry.) The first form of the group's blood-cell measure: an index combining the activity of the cell's energy-making enzymes with the count of mitochondrial DNA copies, measured in white blood cells. In 91 mothers (46 caring for a child with autism, 45 not), the index was lower in the caregivers and higher after happier days, and it did better than any single mitochondrial measure.
*Why not logged:* it shows the index responds to mood and stress. It does not follow anyone forward to an outcome, so it does not meet the test of the entry proposed below, and it is not yet a tool anyone could use to say a repair worked.

**6. Rausser and others, eLife 10: e70899, 2021-10-26** (PMC8612706, https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.70899). Found while looking for the group's blood work. In 21 healthy adults aged 20 to 59, the group measured mitochondrial features in sorted white-cell types and in mixed cells. Platelets (blood fragments that contain mitochondrial DNA but no cell nucleus) that stick to the sample made the measures read 9 to 22 per cent high. Platelet count falls about 6 per cent per decade of life in their sample, which, they note, could account for the fall in blood mitochondrial DNA with age seen in earlier studies. In the sorted cell types mitochondrial DNA per cell rose with age (about 10 per cent per decade in two types), the opposite direction, and these differences were hidden in mixed cells. They say larger studies are needed.
*Why not logged now:* it bears on whether a blood count of mitochondrial DNA is a clean measure of anything about ageing, which only matters if the proposed entry below is accepted. If it is, this paper and the next belong in the log with it.

**7. Picard, Mitochondrion 60: 1-11, 2021** ("Blood mitochondrial DNA copy number: what are we counting?", PMC8464495, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mito.2021.06.010). A review. It says that the blood count of mitochondrial DNA is mostly set by which kinds of white cells and how many platelets are in the sample, that it is not a marker of how well mitochondria work or of how many are being made, that a high or a low count can each mean trouble, and that studies should record platelets and white-cell types or use purified cells.
*Why not logged now:* as above, it is the main caution on the proposed entry.

**The group's other aging papers**, listed on picardlab.org and seen by title only, were not read and are not judged here: Sturm and others, Communications Biology 2023, and Bobba-Alves and others, Psychoneuroendocrinology 2023 (both cultured skin cells and mitochondrial disease); Kurade and others, Nature Metabolism 2025, on a stress hormone (FGF21); Shaulson and others, Nature Aging 2024, a model paper. All are cell, patient-cohort or conceptual work; none is on its face a treatment or a validated tool, but that is a judgment from the titles. A follow-up reading could open them.

## The measuring-age branch: can a blood mitochondrial entry be written?

**Yes, in one form.** The group's own measures (the health index, the sorted-cell panels, the blood markers still in preprint) have no report that follows people to an outcome, so none can be an entry. But the simplest blood mitochondrial measure, the number of copies of mitochondrial DNA per cell, has large peer-reviewed outcome reports from other groups, and the group's own review is the main reason for caution. An entry can be written to the project's standard. The draft is below. It is a proposal for John's ruling, so it is not in `data/`.

What the settling reports say (all read in their open copies at PubMed Central, except the meta-analysis, read in its abstract):
- Ashar and others, Journal of Molecular Medicine 93: 177-186 (online 2014-12-04; https://doi.org/10.1007/s00109-014-1233-3). 16,401 people in two long-running studies (Cardiovascular Health Study, 4,892; Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities, 11,509). Count of mitochondrial DNA in whole blood fell with age. The lowest fifth had a 47 per cent higher chance of dying (pooled hazard ratio 1.47, 95% interval 1.33 to 1.62) than the highest fifth, after allowing for age and sex.
- Ashar and others, JAMA Cardiology 2(11): 1247-1255 (2017-11; https://doi.org/10.1001/jamacardio.2017.3683). 21,870 people in three studies (the two above plus the Multiethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), mean follow-up 13.5 years. A one-standard-deviation lower count went with a 23 per cent higher risk of heart disease or stroke (hazard ratio 1.23, interval 1.19 to 1.26), unchanged after allowing for the usual risk factors, and added to a standard risk equation it improved how people were sorted into risk groups.
- Fu and others, QJM 118(1): 5-15 (2025-01-01; https://doi.org/10.1093/qjmed/hcae208), abstract only: a review pooling 22 studies found the lowest counts went with higher risk of cardiovascular disease (relative risk 2.09) and of death from any cause (1.33).
- Against it, Rausser 2021 and Picard 2021 (above): neither of the two Ashar reports, as far as I could find in their text, allowed for platelet count or the mix of white cells, and the platelet effect alone could produce part of the link with age.

### Draft entry (for John's ruling; not in `data/`)

    id = "a-blood-count-of-mitochondrial-dna-predicts-death-and-disease"
    name = "A count of mitochondrial DNA in blood predicts a person's death and disease"
    branch = "measuring-age"
    description = """
    Mitochondria, the cell's power plants, carry a little DNA of their own, and the number of copies
    per cell can be counted in a blood sample. In 2014 a research group reported, in over sixteen
    thousand people followed for years, that those with the lowest counts were more likely to die,
    and in 2017 the same group reported that low counts went with more heart disease and stroke in
    over twenty-one thousand. The count is cheap and can be read from stored blood. But a review by a
    mitochondria research group warns that it mostly reflects which blood cells and how many
    platelets a sample holds, not how well the power plants work, and no study found shows it moving
    when a treatment works. It is a research measure, not a test anyone can order."""
    test = """
    The number of copies of mitochondrial DNA per cell, counted in a blood sample, predicts time to
    death or to heart or blood-vessel disease after allowing for age and sex, in two or more
    independent groups of at least a thousand people followed for years, in peer-reviewed reports.
    For the clinic rung: a regulator clears the count as a medical test, or a named health system
    uses it in routine care and publishes that practice. For wide use: offered in routine care in
    several countries."""
    settler = "Peer-reviewed cohort reports; the regulator's clearance record, or a named health system's published practice, for the clinic rung"
    stage = "first-human"
    stage_reached_on = "2014-12-04"
    stage_record = "https://doi.org/10.1007/s00109-014-1233-3"
    stage_publication = "ashar-2015"
    status = "tracked"
    horizon = "mid"

    [estimate]
    status = "first-pass"
    set_on = 2026-10-18
    why = """
    The reports are large and agree on direction, but a standard web search on 2026-10-18 found no
    regulator clearing the count and no health system using it (the regulator's own device
    database was not opened). The one measurement problem the group raises, platelets and the
    mix of white cells, is not settled in the large reports and would have to be before a
    laboratory could sell the count as a test; and no report found shows the count moving with a
    treatment, which a measure for judging repairs needs. The earliest year assumes a health
    system publishes use of the count inside a risk score within five years; the latest, 2100,
    means perhaps not this century, because the count may stay a research measure."""

    [[estimate.rung]]
    rung = "in-clinical-use"
    earliest = 2031
    likely = 2045
    latest = 2100

If John rules yes, these go into `data/papers/` with the entry, each with `moved_range = false`: `ashar-2015` (`settled_stage = true`, the entry's stage record), and `ashar-2017`, `rausser-2021` and `picard-2021` (`settled_stage = false`). Fu 2025 is logged only after its full text is opened. The draft above was run through the validator in a throwaway copy on 2026-10-18 and passed.

### The proposal, with the rule of 2026-09-08

| | |
|---|---|
| **The choice** | Add one entry to the measuring branch for the blood count of mitochondrial DNA, at "first human", with a first-pass clinic range of 2031, 2045, 2100. The group's own measures (the health index, sorted-cell panels, blood markers GDF15 and cell-free mitochondrial DNA) are left off until an outcome report in peer review exists. |
| **Confidence** | Moderate that the entry meets the project's standard (plain test, peer-reviewed settler, dated stage record): high on that. Low to moderate on the estimate, which is a judgment with one search behind the "no clearance" claim. |
| **Standard or judgment** | The test and stage follow the pattern of the existing blood-clock entry. The decision to put a measure with a known confound on the tree at all is a judgment call. |
| **Strongest alternative** | Write the test so it requires the reports to allow for platelets and white-cell mix. That is more honest about what the count measures, but no large report meets it, so the entry would have no stage record above "idea only" and the ladder, which counts animal and human results, does not fit a measure that has been in people since 2014. |
| **Would I change it on reflection?** | Possibly the name: "a count of mitochondrial DNA" is accurate but not obviously "a measure of ageing"; the entry's description says it is not a test of how well mitochondria work. |
