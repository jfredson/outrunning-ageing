# The eight branches

Every branch is marked required, and a branch counts as handled in the year its second treatment reaches clinical use (`handled_when = 2`). The run's escape year is the latest handled year across all eight, plus the drawn lag. The treatments per branch are counted below; the ones in this packet are given in full further down.

- **Worn-out cells** (`worn-out-cells`): Cells that have stopped dividing but will not die, and sit in the tissue giving off signals that inflame their neighbours. They build up with age; clearing them makes old mice healthier and longer-lived. Treatments tracked: 5, of which 0 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A controlled trial in people in which a drug that kills these cells improves an age-related condition on its primary outcome, or a regulator's approval of one.
- **Damaged DNA** (`damaged-dna`): Mutations that accumulate in a cell's DNA over a lifetime, and the shortening of the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes each time a cell divides. Treatments tracked: 4, of which 0 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A gene therapy for a single ageing-related mutation with a measured result in people, or a treatment that lengthens chromosome ends in a living animal with no rise in cancer.
- **The cell's settings** (`cell-settings`): The chemical marks that tell a cell which of its genes to read, which drift with age so that cells slowly forget what they are. Resetting them part of the way has reversed signs of age in animals. Treatments tracked: 5, of which 0 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A measured reversal of a marker of biological age in a person after a brief, controlled dose of reprogramming genes, in a peer-reviewed report.
- **Broken proteins and waste** (`broken-proteins`): Misfolded proteins, the failure of the cell's recycling of its own worn parts, and the waste and cross-links between proteins that build up inside and between cells. Treatments tracked: 5, of which 2 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A drug that clears a kind of waste throughout the body, not one organ, with a healthy-years or function result in a controlled trial in people.
- **Energy and fuel sensing** (`energy-and-fuel`): The cell's power plants, which fail with age, and the pathways that sense how much food is coming in, which run wrong with age and are the target of the drugs most often said to slow ageing. Treatments tracked: 8, of which 1 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A controlled trial in people in which a drug that acts on these pathways keeps the people taking it free of age-related disease longer than those on placebo, by the margin the trial was designed to detect.
- **Replacing lost cells and parts** (`replacing-parts`): The reserve of cells that replace lost tissue runs out with age, tissue goes unreplaced and organs fail. The route around that is to supply the parts: stem cells, organs from gene-edited animals, organs grown outside the body. Treatments tracked: 7, of which 1 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A regulator's approval of an organ from a gene-edited animal or an organ grown from a person's own cells for use outside a trial, or a stem cell therapy restoring a lost function in a controlled trial.
- **Inflammation and the body's messaging** (`inflammation-and-signalling`): Low-grade inflammation that never switches off, the gut's community of microbes going out of balance, and the signals cells send each other through the blood going wrong with age. Treatments tracked: 5, of which 1 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A measured effect in people of exchanging or diluting the blood's plasma, or an anti-inflammatory drug with a healthy-years result in a controlled trial.
- **Measuring ageing** (`measuring-age`): The tools that say whether any of the above worked without waiting decades: biological age clocks read from blood or saliva, blood and imaging markers, and whether a regulator will accept any of them as proof that a treatment slowed ageing. Treatments tracked: 5, of which 0 already in clinical use. What would move this branch: A regulator accepting a marker of ageing as a trial endpoint, or recognising ageing itself as a condition a drug can be approved to treat.

## Treatment 1 of 9 in this packet: A drug lengthens the ends of chromosomes in a person
Record id: `a-drug-lengthens-chromosome-ends-in-a-person`. Branch: `damaged-dna`. Horizon the record gives: mid.

### What it is
The caps on chromosome ends (telomeres) shorten with each cell division and with age, and some
people inherit faults that shorten them fast, so their bone marrow and lungs fail young. In 2016
a National Institutes of Health group gave such people danazol, a synthetic hormone that had been
on the market for decades, and measured their telomeres: in eleven of the twelve people who
reached two years, the caps had grown longer, and most patients' blood counts improved. It is the
first measured lengthening of telomeres in people by a drug. The drug is not approved for the
purpose, a long-term follow-up published in 2026 measured telomere length again, by a different method, in the few patients with usable samples, and found it stable, with no significant lengthening, and it was given for a rare disease,
not for ageing.

### What would count (the test)
A person given a treatment afterwards has measurably longer telomeres (the caps on chromosome
ends) in their blood cells than before, in a peer-reviewed report with the measurement method
stated. For the clinic rung: a regulator approves a treatment for lengthening telomeres, or for a
telomere disease on the basis of lengthening them; off-label use does not count.

Who or what settles it: The peer-reviewed report; the regulator's approval record for the clinic rung

## Treatment 2 of 9 in this packet: A gene is edited inside a person's body to stop an age-related disease
Record id: `a-gene-is-edited-inside-a-persons-body`. Branch: `damaged-dna`. Horizon the record gives: near.

### What it is
Most gene editing so far has been done on cells taken out of the body and put back. Editing a
gene inside a living person is the step that would let the damage of ageing be repaired where it
sits. In 2021 a London group gave six people a single infusion of a CRISPR gene editor (a tool that cuts DNA
at a chosen spot) packaged in fat droplets, which went to the liver and switched off the gene for transthyretin, a protein that
misfolds and clogs the heart and nerves in an inherited disease and, in its ordinary form, in many
old people. The protein fell by about nine tenths and stayed down. The treatment is in a large
late-stage trial for the heart form of the disease, which was paused in late 2025 after a
patient's liver was harmed, and restarted in March 2026 with closer liver monitoring; nothing is approved yet. That
patient died on 2025-11-05. The company's report to the securities regulator of 2026-01-09 says the trial's lead doctor reported
death from septic shock (a dangerous collapse of blood pressure caused by infection) after a
perforated duodenal ulcer (a hole through the wall of the first part of the small intestine), that the
patient's course also included acute liver injury and its treatment with steroids, and that an
autopsy supported the clinical diagnoses.

A person is given a treatment that edits a gene inside their body, and afterwards the gene's
product is measurably changed in the way intended, in a peer-reviewed report. For the clinic rung:
a regulator approves a gene-editing treatment given inside the body for an age-related disease,
or for a disease of DNA damage, so a doctor can give it outside a trial.

## Treatment 3 of 9 in this packet: A gene therapy lengthens the ends of chromosomes in an old animal, with no rise in cancer
Record id: `gene-therapy-lengthens-chromosome-ends-in-an-old-animal`. Branch: `damaged-dna`. Horizon the record gives: far.

### What it is
Each time a cell divides, the protective caps on the ends of its chromosomes (telomeres) get a
little shorter, and a cell whose caps run out stops dividing. An enzyme called telomerase rebuilds
the caps, and most adult cells keep it switched off, partly because cancers switch it on. In 2012
a Madrid group carried the telomerase gene into adult and old mice with a harmless virus, once,
and the mice lived longer (by about a quarter when treated at one year old, about an eighth at
two) without more cancer. The leaf asks for that result and for its safety, because a longer life
with more cancer would not count.

### What would count (the test)
A living animal given a treatment that lengthens its telomeres (the caps on chromosome ends)
afterwards lives longer, or stays healthier for longer, than untreated controls, with no rise in
cancer, in a peer-reviewed report. The first-human rung needs measurably longer telomeres in a
person after such a gene therapy, with the cancer rate reported, in a peer-reviewed report. For
the clinic rung: a regulator approves a telomere-lengthening gene therapy for any condition.

Who or what settles it: Peer-reviewed reports; the trial registry and report when a first person is treated; the regulator's approval record for the clinic rung

## Treatment 4 of 9 in this packet: The mutation behind a premature-ageing disease is corrected in a living animal
Record id: `premature-ageing-mutation-corrected-in-a-living-animal`. Branch: `damaged-dna`. Horizon the record gives: mid.

### What it is
Progeria is a disease in which one wrong letter in one gene makes children age fast and die in
their teens. In 2021 a Harvard and National Institutes of Health group used a base editor, a tool
that changes a single letter of DNA without cutting it, carried into mice with the human mutation
by a harmless virus. One dose corrected the letter in roughly a fifth to three-fifths of the cells in various organs
(about a quarter in the main artery) six months later, and the mice lived about 2.4 times as
long as untreated mice. It is the clearest case
of repairing a single ageing-related mutation in a living body. The children's version of the
disease has an approved drug (lonafarnib, 2020) that treats its effects, but no person has had the
mutation itself corrected.

### What would count (the test)
A living animal carrying a mutation that causes premature ageing, or an ageing-related disease,
has the mutation corrected in a substantial share of the cells of an affected organ by a treatment
given after birth, and lives longer or stays healthier for it, in a peer-reviewed report. The
first-human rung is the same correction, measured, in a person. For the clinic rung: a regulator
approves a gene-correction therapy for such a disease.

Who or what settles it: Peer-reviewed reports; the trial registry and report when a first person is treated; the regulator's approval record for the clinic rung

# From the method document: what the computation does and the parameters

## What the computation does

1. **Every tracked leaf supplies a year it reaches the clinic** (`in-clinical-use`). A leaf already there, or beyond, supplies the year it happened. A leaf below draws a year from its estimate: a triangle over the earliest, likely and latest years, so the likely year is the most probable draw and the ends are possible but rare (`docs/record-schema.md`, "How a leaf's state feeds the headline").
2. **Every branch counts as handled in the year its `handled_when`-th leaf reaches the clinic.** With the default of two, a branch is handled when its second treatment is in clinical use. The branch's leaf years are sorted and the second is taken.
3. **The run's escape year is the latest handled year across the branches marked `required`, plus a drawn lag** for the treatments to spread from the clinic to ordinary people, so that the year is when a person who keeps up with the best available care actually gets the gain, not when the paper came out.
4. **Many thousands of runs**, each with fresh draws, give a spread of escape years. The headline reads three numbers off it: the median year; the share of runs with an escape year before 2050; the share of runs with an escape year after 2100, said in public as "not this century". The 5th and 95th percentiles are reported as the spread ("anywhere from 2044 to not this century" when the 95th is past 2100).

Two honesty rules from the kickoff, repeated on the public method page: the combination rule (all required branches handled, plus a lag) is the project's judgment, and the strongest alternative, a weighted sum in which some branches matter more, is recorded below with why it was not chosen; and the ranges are the project's reading of the evidence, revised only with a dated reason and a source, never because a number looked wrong.

## A worked run, by hand

For the method page, once the leaves exist, one simplified run is written out so a reader can follow it: eight branches, two leaves each, one drawn year per leaf, the second-smallest year per branch, the largest of the eight, plus the lag. Until then this is the shape of it:
