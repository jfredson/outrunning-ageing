## What happened

On 9 March 2026 a group led from [Brigham and Women's Hospital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_and_Women%27s_Hospital) in Boston published, in Nature Medicine, [a clock study of the COSMOS trial](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04239-3). In [COSMOS](https://cosmostrial.org/), older adults took a daily multivitamin, a cocoa extract, both, or dummy pills. In 958 healthy participants, about 70 years old on average, the group read five clocks from the marks on the DNA in their blood, at the start and after one and two years ([the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04239-3)). The multivitamin slowed two of the five, the two trained to predict death and disease, by about 0.11 and 0.21 years for each year of the trial. The cocoa extract slowed none.

The [weekly reading of 18 October](/reading/#2026-10-18) found the paper, and the site logged it the same day from its abstract. The question of whether it should replace the entry's record was left for the author. The full paper was read on 19 October, and he ruled the same day that it should.

## Why

The entry's test asks for a controlled trial in people, with the clock analysis planned before the data were unblinded, reporting in a peer-reviewed paper that names the clock that a treatment slowed it ([the entry](/leaves/a-treatment-slows-a-biological-age-clock-in-a-controlled-trial/)). The 2023 [CALERIE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CALERIE) result, where cutting calories slowed a clock of the pace of ageing, was the entry's first record. But [its paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-022-00357-y) calls its clock analysis post hoc, meaning decided after the fact, so the site ruled on 17 October that it does not meet the test. The COSMOS paper says its clock study was prespecified before the main trial's data were unlocked. That is the closest any report on the site comes to the test, so it is now the record. The entry was already at first human, so no rung moves. The stage date moves to the day this paper was published.

## What it does not mean

It does not mean a multivitamin slows ageing. The paper's own authors call the effects small and say more studies are needed to know whether they matter for health ([the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-026-04239-3)). It does not mean the test is met beyond doubt, either:

- The paper gives no date for its plan. Its [trial registration](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05510375) was first submitted after the treatments had ended.
- It does not show that the reworked, steadier versions of the clocks its result rests on were chosen in advance.
- It made no allowance for testing five clocks, and its authors say some false results are likely.

The entry's page says all of this, and a later report that settles these points would replace this one.
